In eight questions

What is the problem?

Fungi run forest growth, soil carbon and much of our medicine, yet fewer than 1 in 10 species are known. The richest places, tropical rainforests, are the least sampled. The best global map is 1 km per pixel, and satellite maps like TESSERA give one summary a year, while fungi appear and vanish within days.

What is the solution?

Low-cost passive samplers catch fungal spores from the air into a sealed weekly cartridge; DNA names the species, including unnamed ones. We pair each sample with free 10 m satellite embeddings and train a model that predicts fungi everywhere, shows where it is unsure, and sends the next sampler there.

Biggest limitation?

The core claim is unproven. On 66 real UK woodlands, satellite data did not beat soil pH. Our tropical result is a simulation. Guided placement only helps when samples are scarce. Week-long passive sampling in humid understory is untested. The pilot has a pass mark set in advance; if we miss it, we stop.

What is the economic gap?

  • Pharma: $0.7B to $2.2B per approved drug. Blind collecting rediscovers known compounds and carries legal risk. Nobody sells "where to collect, with consent attached".
  • Governments: must report on biodiversity, with almost no soil-life data.
  • Fieldwork is the cost, not the lab (about $4 chemistry, ~$150 to $500 commercial per sample). Guided placement makes each trip count.

Who is the payer?

Mission: ecosystem sustainability. Revenue: pharma keeps it running.

  1. Governments and agencies: national fungal monitoring for biodiversity reporting.
  2. Restoration and conservation: proof soil life is recovering, measured not assumed.
  3. eDNA and Earth-observation firms such as EchoLabs: partners and data buyers.
  4. Pharma and biotech (sustains the company): collection shortlists with consent and permits; part of the revenue returns to source communities.
  5. Later: farm biologicals, then insurance once the map is proven.

Ecological risk, if any?

  • Over-collecting: sensitive locations are blurred or withheld.
  • False absence: uncertainty is always published, so "nothing here" can't justify clearing.
  • Biopiracy: communities co-own the data under Nagoya and the Cali Fund.
  • Biosecurity: strict rules for samples that may carry pathogens.
  • Footprint: passive, no chemicals, every unit retrieved.

What data do we need?

  • Weekly airborne fungal DNA from fixed tropical sites: the missing piece.
  • Satellite embeddings (TESSERA, AlphaEarth): free.
  • Elevation, climate, soil (Copernicus, WorldClim, SoilGrids): free.
  • Benchmarks: SPUN 1 km map, GlobalFungi, Global Soil Mycobiome, Global Spore Sampling Project: open.
  • Ground checks: soil and fruiting-body surveys at the same sites.

What dataset becomes our moat?

A multi-year, weekly, DNA-identified record of airborne fungi at fixed tropical sites, each sample tied to its location, date, satellite habitat and signed consent.

  • Time can't be bought: a later competitor never gets this year's seasons.
  • Consent takes years: agreements with countries and communities aren't signed overnight.
  • Nobody else builds it: existing efforts are temperate, soil-based or one-off.

Satellite data, models and sampler hardware are open to everyone. They are not the moat.

How it works

Catch the DNA. Read the land. Learn. Choose the next spot.

1Catch

Low-cost passive samplers pull fungal spores out of the air into a sealed weekly cartridge. Sequencing names what was there, including species nobody has named.

2Read

Free 10 m satellite embeddings (TESSERA, AlphaEarth) describe every pixel: trees, water, structure. Radar sees through cloud.

3Learn

A model links habitat to the fungi found there and predicts the places we have not sampled, with an honest uncertainty map.

4Choose

The next sampler goes where the model is least sure. Each sample teaches the model as much as possible.

Wider impacts and safeguards
  • Location secrecy. Rare species and chemistry hotspots are blurred or withheld, so maps can't guide over-collecting.
  • No false "empty". We always publish uncertainty. A blank prediction is never sold as proof that nothing lives there.
  • Bias in the open. The model is weakest where data is thinnest. We report accuracy per region.
  • Consent and sharing. Communities and host institutions co-own the data (CARE principles), are paid partners and co-authors, and data stays in-country.
  • Biosecurity. Air samples can hold plant and human pathogens. Handling, shipping and release rules come first.
  • Light footprint. Passive, no chemicals released, every unit retrieved or recycled.
What we must prove
  • 90 days: 10 m embeddings plus dated labels beat the 1 km map and a climate-only model on tropical sites, with the pass mark set in advance.
  • 6 months: 10 pilot sites with open-source samplers, about $15k to $45k. Measure real cost per sample and whether cartridges survive humid understory.
  • 12 months: one paying customer before any custom hardware.
  • Always: cost per unit of model improvement falls as we grow.
